The 10 Easiest Vegetables to Grow for Beginners

Your first garden should be fun, and it should feed you. The trick is choosing crops that grow fast, forgive a missed watering and don't need special skills. These ten do all three.

What makes a vegetable easy? It grows from seed sown straight in the garden, so you don't need grow lights. It doesn't get many pests or diseases. It tolerates a little neglect. And it rewards you quickly, so you see results while you're still excited.

Here are ten that fit, roughly in order from easiest to slightly more effort. Pick three or four for your first season. A small garden you keep up with grows more food than a big one you don't.

1. Radishes

Ready in: about 4 weeks from seed
Plant: early spring and again in fall

The fastest crop in the garden. Sow seeds half an inch deep straight in the soil and you'll be pulling radishes in about a month. Sow a short row every couple of weeks for a steady supply. They turn hot and woody in summer heat, so grow them in the cool months.

2. Lettuce and salad greens

Ready in: 4 to 8 weeks
Plant: spring and fall

Loose-leaf lettuce lets you pick the outer leaves and keep the plant growing for weeks. It grows in a bed, a pot or a window box, and likes some afternoon shade when it gets warm. Sow a little every two weeks and you'll rarely buy salad.

3. Bush beans

Ready in: about 8 weeks
Plant: after your last frost, when the soil is warm

Bush beans need no trellis and very little care. Push the seeds an inch deep into warm soil, water them, and stand back. The more you pick, the more they make, so pick every couple of days once they start.

4. Zucchini

Ready in: 7 to 8 weeks
Plant: after your last frost, once nights are warm

One or two plants are plenty for a family. Zucchini grows fast and produces for months. Pick the fruits when they're 6 to 8 inches long; they turn huge and watery if you miss a few days. Give each plant about a yard of space.

5. Cherry tomatoes

Ready in: about 9 weeks after planting out
Plant: as seedlings, once nights stay above 50°F

Cherry tomatoes are far easier than big slicing tomatoes. They ripen sooner, crack less and keep producing until frost. Buy a healthy seedling, plant it deep in a sunny spot, give it a stake or cage, and water steadily.

6. Peas

Ready in: 8 to 10 weeks
Plant: early spring, as soon as the soil can be worked

Peas are one of the first things you can plant in spring, and they like the cold. Give them a short fence or some twine to climb. Snap peas and snow peas are the easiest because you eat the whole pod.

7. Kale

Ready in: 7 to 9 weeks
Plant: spring and late summer

Kale shrugs off cold and keeps growing well into winter in many places, getting sweeter after a frost. Pick the lower leaves and let the top keep growing; one plant can feed you for months.

8. Swiss chard

Ready in: about 8 weeks
Plant: spring

Chard handles heat better than spinach and cold better than lettuce, so it grows nearly all season. The rainbow varieties look beautiful in a bed. Pick outer leaves as needed.

9. Cucumbers

Ready in: 8 to 9 weeks
Plant: after your last frost, once the soil is warm

Give cucumbers a trellis or fence to climb and they'll take up little space and stay clean. They need steady water; uneven watering makes them bitter. Pick them small and often.

10. Basil

Ready in: 6 to 8 weeks
Plant: once nights are warm

The easiest herb that you'll actually use a lot of. Pinch off the top pair of leaves regularly and the plant grows bushier instead of flowering. Grow it next to your tomatoes and you have half a dinner.

Start with the right timing Half of these crops like cool weather and half like heat. Peas, radishes, lettuce, kale and chard go in early spring. Beans, zucchini, tomatoes, cucumbers and basil wait until after your last frost, once the nights warm up. Planting at the right time for where you live matters more than anything else on this list.

A few tips that make everything easier

  • Find your sun. Most vegetables want at least six hours of direct sun. Leafy greens can manage with a little less.
  • Start small. One raised bed or a few big pots is a great first garden.
  • Water deeply, not daily. Check the soil two inches down. If it's dry, water well.
  • Pick often. Beans, zucchini, cucumbers and herbs all produce more when you keep picking.

The short version

For a first garden, choose three or four crops from this list that you love to eat, plant them at the right time for your area, and keep up with watering and picking. By midsummer you'll be eating something you grew almost every day.
