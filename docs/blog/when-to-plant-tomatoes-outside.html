When Is It Warm Enough to Plant Tomatoes Outside?

Most guides say to plant tomatoes "after your last frost." That keeps them alive, but it doesn't make them grow. Tomatoes, peppers and the other heat lovers need warm nights and warm soil too, and waiting a week or two for both gets you a bigger harvest, sooner.

Every spring, gardeners rush tomato plants into the ground the weekend after their last frost date. The plants survive, but then they sit there, pale and stalled, for weeks. Meanwhile a neighbor who planted two weeks later passes them by.

The reason is simple. Avoiding frost keeps a tomato alive. Warmth is what makes it grow. Here's how to tell when you have both.

Your last frost date is the earliest day, not the best day

A last frost date is an average: the date after which a frost is unlikely in your area. It's a useful line for anything that dies in a frost. But tomatoes, peppers, eggplant, squash, cucumbers, melons and basil are all warm-weather crops from warm places, and "not freezing" is far from what they want.

Plant them into cold soil and chilly nights and they stop growing. Roots barely spread, leaves turn purple or yellow, and the plant spends its energy just holding on. Once that happens, it can take weeks to recover.

The two checks that matter

1. Nights reliably above 50°F

For tomatoes, the most useful number is the nighttime low. When nights stay at or above about 50°F, tomatoes grow steadily. Below that, growth slows to a crawl, and a run of nights in the low 40s can set young plants back for weeks.

Peppers and eggplant are fussier still. They want nights closer to 55°F before they really take off. Melons and okra like it even warmer.

Look at the forecast lows for the next week or two, not just tomorrow. One warm night followed by a cold snap doesn't count.

2. Soil at least 60°F

Roots care about soil temperature, and soil warms much more slowly than air. A sunny 75°F afternoon in spring can still leave the soil at 52°F a few inches down. A cheap soil thermometer pushed in about 4 inches, read in the morning, tells you the truth.

Tomatoes want soil around 60°F or warmer. Peppers, eggplant, cucumbers and squash do best at 65°F and up.

These are good-enough targets, not hard lines. A degree or two either way won't ruin anything.

A rule of thumb In much of the US, nights warm up enough for tomatoes about one to three weeks after the last frost date. In places with cool springs, like the Pacific Northwest or along the coast, the gap can be longer. In the Deep South it can be almost nothing.

Get your seedlings ready first

Seedlings grown indoors have never felt wind, full sun or a cool night. Moving them straight outside can scorch the leaves or shock them into stalling. Harden them off over 7 to 10 days: a couple of hours outside in the shade at first, then a little longer and a little more sun each day, bringing them in on cold nights.

If you started seeds too early and your plants are getting leggy while you wait for warm nights, move them to a bigger pot. They'll keep growing roots instead of stretching.

How to plant earlier, if you want to

You can cheat the calendar a little by warming things up yourself:

  • Cover the soil a week ahead. Black plastic or landscape fabric laid over the bed warms the soil several degrees.
  • Use a raised bed. Raised beds warm up faster in spring than ground-level soil.
  • Protect the plants at night. Row cover, cloches or water-filled plant protectors hold in the day's warmth.
  • Plant tomatoes deep. Bury the stem up to the top few sets of leaves. Tomatoes grow roots all along the buried stem, so the plant gets a bigger root system in slightly warmer soil.

What happens if a cold night sneaks in

If the forecast drops below 50°F after you've planted, don't panic. Throw a sheet or row cover over the plants in the evening and take it off in the morning. If frost is forecast, cover them fully, and water the soil the day before, since moist soil holds heat better than dry soil.

Tomatoes that get a cold night or two will usually recover. What sets them back is weeks of cold, which is exactly what waiting for warm nights avoids.

The short version

Use your last frost date to know when the danger is over, then wait for nights above 50°F and soil above 60°F before planting tomatoes. Give peppers, eggplant and melons a little longer. The plants you put in a week or two later will grow faster, flower sooner and give you more to pick.
