How to Store Fresh Vegetables From Your Garden

Growing it is the hard part. Then a tomato goes mealy in the fridge or a bunch of herbs turns black in two days. Where you keep each crop matters as much as when you pick it, and most of it comes down to a few simple rules.

Homegrown food has one big advantage over the store: it's picked at its peak and hasn't spent a week on a truck. Keep it the right way and it stays that good for days, sometimes months. Keep it the wrong way and you lose that advantage overnight.

Five rules that cover most crops

  • Pick in the morning. Leaves and fruit are cool and full of water after the night. Afternoon picks wilt faster.
  • Don't wash until you're about to eat it. Water left on berries, greens and herbs is what makes them go soft and moldy. Brush off dirt, then store.
  • Cut the leafy tops off root crops. Beet, carrot, radish and turnip greens keep pulling moisture out of the root. Cut them off, leaving an inch of stem, and store them separately. Most of them are good to eat.
  • Know what wants cold and what doesn't. Leaves, roots and most berries want the fridge. Warm-weather fruits like tomatoes, basil and winter squash are damaged by it.
  • Give stems water. Anything you'd put in a vase, like herbs, green onions or asparagus, keeps longer standing in an inch of water.

Keep on the counter

Tomatoes

Keep them on the counter, stem side down, out of direct sun. The fridge turns the flesh mealy and dulls the flavor. Only refrigerate a tomato once it's fully ripe and you can't eat it in the next day or two, then let it warm up before eating.

Basil

Basil blackens in the cold. Trim the stems and stand them in a glass of water on the counter, like flowers. It keeps about a week.

Melons, peaches and pears

Let them finish ripening on the counter. Pears are picked firm and ripen indoors; they're ready when the neck gives a little. Once ripe, move peaches, pears and cut melon to the fridge.

Keep in a cool, dark, dry place

A cupboard, basement or garage that stays cool but doesn't freeze is ideal for crops meant to last for months.

Potatoes

Brush off the dirt, don't wash them, and keep them in a paper bag or box in the dark. Light turns them green. Keep them away from onions, which make them sprout faster.

Onions and garlic

Cure them first: dry them somewhere airy and out of the sun for two to three weeks, until the necks are papery and dry. Then keep them in a mesh bag or basket in a cool, dry spot. Well-cured onions and garlic keep for months.

Winter squash and pumpkins

Cure them in a warm, sunny spot for a week or two to harden the skin, then keep them somewhere cool and dry. Butternut and pumpkins can last three months or more. Leave a few inches of stem on; a squash without a stem rots quickly.

Sweet potatoes

Cure them somewhere warm and humid for about a week, which sweetens them and heals any nicks, then keep them cool and dark, but never in the fridge.

Keep in the fridge

Lettuce, spinach, kale and other greens

Wrap them loosely in a damp paper towel or cloth and put them in a bag or container. Lettuce keeps about a week, sturdy kale and chard a little longer.

Carrots, beets, radishes and turnips

Cut off the tops, then keep the roots in a bag in the crisper drawer. Carrots and beets keep for weeks this way. If carrots go limp, a soak in cold water perks them back up.

Herbs like parsley, cilantro and dill

Stand them in a jar with an inch of water, cover loosely with a bag, and keep them in the fridge. Or wrap them in a damp towel. Either way they keep about a week.

Green onions and asparagus

Stand them upright in a glass with an inch of water in the fridge. Asparagus loses its sweetness fast, so eat it within three days.

Cucumbers, zucchini and peppers

Keep them in the crisper drawer, dry, and use them within a week. Cucumbers and zucchini don't like the very coldest spot, so the front of the drawer is better than the back.

Beans and peas

Keep them in a bag in the fridge and eat them within a few days. Peas turn starchy quickly, so the sooner the better.

Strawberries, raspberries and blueberries

Spread them in a single layer on a paper towel, unwashed, and eat them within two or three days. Blueberries last longer, up to a week or two.

Eat it today

A few crops are best the day you pick them. Sweet corn starts turning its sugar to starch within hours. Peas and asparagus lose sweetness fast too. Plan to cook these the same day, or pick them just before dinner.

Too much at once? Most crops freeze well. Berries, cherries and sliced peaches freeze on a tray first, then go into a bag. Greens, beans and peas do best blanched for a minute or two in boiling water, then cooled in ice water before freezing. Herbs freeze well chopped in a little oil in an ice cube tray.

The short version

Cut the tops off roots, keep greens in a damp towel in the fridge, keep tomatoes and basil out of it, cure onions, garlic and squash before storing them somewhere cool and dark, and wash nothing until you eat it. Do that, and far less of what you grow goes to waste.
