When to Fertilize Fruit Trees (and When Not To)

More fertilizer isn't automatically better. Feed some fruit trees too generously and you get lush leaves and no fruit at all; here's what each one actually wants.

Fruit trees get treated like they all want the same feeding schedule, but their needs vary more than most gardeners assume, both by species and by how established the tree is. Getting it wrong in either direction, too much or too little, shows up the same way: a tree that isn't fruiting the way you expected.

The general rule: feed during active growth, stop before frost

Almost every fruit tree wants feeding concentrated in its active growing season, typically spring through mid-to-late summer, and wants that feeding to stop a few weeks before your first fall frost. Fertilizing late in the season pushes tender new growth right as cold weather approaches, and that new growth is exactly what's most vulnerable to frost damage. A tree that stopped growing for the season and hardened off is far better prepared for winter than one still pushing soft new shoots.

Citrus (lemon, lime, orange, kumquat)

Citrus is a heavy feeder. Feed monthly with a balanced citrus fertilizer during active growth, or quarterly with a slow-release granular version if you'd rather feed less often. Citrus specifically benefits from the micronutrients (iron, zinc, manganese) in citrus-formulated fertilizer, which address the yellowing leaf patterns citrus is prone to when those are lacking.

Figs

Figs are the opposite of citrus: they barely need feeding at all, and overfeeding is the more common mistake. Too much nitrogen pushes vigorous leaf growth at the direct expense of fruit production. Feed lightly, if at all, once established, and never assume more fertilizer will fix a fig that isn't fruiting well; it more likely needs the opposite.

Avocado

Avocado wants regular feeding through the growing season, but is notably sensitive to salt buildup in the soil, which shows up as browned leaf tips. Water deeply between feedings to help flush excess salts, and avoid over-fertilizing, which compounds the problem rather than helping.

Olive

Olive is drought-tolerant and a relatively light feeder once established, closer to fig than to citrus in its needs. Overfeeding an olive tends to produce excess leafy growth without improving fruit set.

Pomegranate

Pomegranate is fairly low-maintenance and drought-tolerant once established, and doesn't need aggressive feeding. A modest feeding during the growing season is generally enough; more isn't better here either.

Peach and cherry (stone fruit)

Stone fruit trees want more consistent feeding through the growing season than fig or olive, especially while fruit is actively developing and sizing up, since fruit production is genuinely nutrient-demanding for these species.

What "young tree" actually means for fertilizing

A newly planted tree, in roughly its first year, is focused on establishing roots, not producing fruit, and may give little to no harvest regardless of how well it's fed. That's normal, not a sign something's wrong. Don't compensate with extra fertilizer; overfeeding a young, unestablished tree can do more harm than good by pushing top growth the young root system can't yet support. Once established, most of these trees shift into a more predictable, full-production pattern in the years that follow.

Blueberries and raspberries follow the same logic They're not trees, but as perennial bushes and canes they follow the same establishment-then-production pattern. Blueberries want an acid-forming fertilizer a couple of times a season (regular fertilizer pushes soil pH the wrong direction for them); raspberries want less feeding and more attention to pruning out old canes after fruiting.

Signs you're overfeeding

  • Lush, vigorous leaf growth with little or no fruit
  • Excessive soft new growth right before your first frost
  • Leaf tip burn (especially in avocado, often a salt buildup issue)

Signs you're underfeeding an established tree

  • Pale or yellowing leaves not explained by watering issues
  • Weak or minimal new growth each season
  • Declining fruit size or quantity in a tree old enough to be in full production
