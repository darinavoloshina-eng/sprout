How Often Should You Water a Vegetable Garden?

"Once or twice a week" is the answer you'll see everywhere, and it's wrong often enough to matter. The real number depends on your soil, your bed, and the weather you're actually having.

There's no single watering schedule that works for every garden, and any answer that doesn't ask about your soil type, bed setup, and climate is guessing. That said, here's the actual logic behind a good watering schedule, so you can work out your own number instead of copying someone else's.

Start with "an inch of water a week," then adjust

The commonly cited baseline is about one inch of water per week for most vegetable gardens, from rain or irrigation combined. That's a reasonable starting point for average conditions: moderate temperatures, decent soil, no wind. It is not a fixed rule. Treat it as the number you adjust from, not the number you follow blindly.

What actually changes the number

Soil type

Sandy soil drains fast and holds almost no water, so it needs more frequent, lighter watering. Clay soil holds water much longer but drains poorly, so it needs less frequent, deeper watering, and overwatering clay is what actually drowns roots. Loam sits in between and is the most forgiving. If you don't know your soil type, squeeze a handful when it's moist: sand won't hold a shape at all, clay holds a sticky ball, loam holds a loose clump that breaks apart easily.

Bed type

Raised beds drain faster and dry out faster than in-ground beds, because they're elevated and usually filled with a lighter, more amended soil mix. A raised bed in summer heat can need watering every day or two, while the same crops in-ground might go three or four days between waterings. Containers dry out fastest of all, sometimes needing water daily in hot weather.

Temperature and wind

Heat and wind both pull moisture out of soil faster through evaporation, and out of plants faster through transpiration. A 95°F week with dry wind can double your watering needs compared to a mild, humid week, even with the same rainfall.

What's actually planted

Shallow-rooted crops like lettuce, radishes, and most leafy greens dry out fast and want frequent, light watering. Deep-rooted crops like tomatoes and squash can go longer between waterings if you water deeply each time, since their roots reach moisture further down. Fruiting crops (tomatoes, peppers, squash) are also far more sensitive to inconsistent watering than leafy greens: irregular watering is the single biggest cause of blossom end rot and split fruit.

The two-inch test Stick a finger two inches into the soil. If it's dry at that depth, water. If it's still moist, wait. This single check beats any fixed schedule, because it responds to what's actually happening in your bed instead of a calendar guess.

Deep and infrequent beats shallow and daily

A light daily sprinkle wets only the top inch of soil and trains roots to stay shallow, which makes plants more vulnerable to drought stress the moment you miss a day. Watering deeply, less often, encourages roots to grow down toward the moisture, building a plant that's more resilient overall. Aim to water until the top 6-8 inches of soil are moist, then let the surface dry out somewhat before watering again (except for shallow-rooted crops, which do want more frequent, lighter watering by nature).

Morning beats evening

Water in the early morning when it's cool, so plants have all day to take it up and foliage has time to dry before evening. Watering at night leaves leaves wet for hours in the dark, which is exactly the condition fungal diseases like powdery mildew and blight prefer.

Signs you're overwatering, not underwatering

Yellowing lower leaves, soft or mushy stems, and soil that stays soggy days after watering all point to overwatering, not drought stress, even though the plant can look wilted either way. Before adding more water to a struggling plant, check the soil first. A wilted plant in wet soil needs less water and better drainage, not more water.
