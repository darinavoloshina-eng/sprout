Does Rain Count as Watering Your Garden?

It rained last night, so do you still need to water? Sometimes the answer is no for days. Sometimes the rain barely reached the roots. Here's how to tell the difference without guessing.

Rain is the best water your garden gets. It's free, it's soft, and it soaks the whole bed evenly. But not every rain counts. A light shower on a warm afternoon can evaporate before it reaches a single root, while a slow, steady half inch can keep a bed moist for days.

How much rain is enough?

Most vegetable gardens need about an inch of water a week, from rain and watering combined. Thirsty crops like tomatoes, squash and cucumbers want more in hot weather, and leafy greens a little less. With that in mind:

  • Under a quarter inch: barely counts. It wets the surface and mostly evaporates. Water as usual.
  • About half an inch: a real soaking. It reaches well into the root zone of most vegetables. You can usually skip watering for two or three days, longer in cool weather.
  • An inch or more: covers most of the week. Skip watering and check the soil again in a few days.

An inch of rain is a lot of water: about six tenths of a gallon on every square foot of your garden, or around 20 gallons on a 4 by 8 foot bed.

Measure it, don't guess Rain is easy to misjudge. A loud ten-minute downpour can drop less than a light, all-afternoon drizzle. A simple rain gauge in the garden, or the rainfall from your local weather report, tells you what actually fell.

Where rain doesn't reach

Even after a good rain, some spots stay dry:

  • Containers and hanging pots. Leaves cover most of the soil, so a pot catches very little rain. Always check pots with your finger.
  • Under eaves and overhangs. Beds against the house often sit in a rain shadow.
  • Under big leaves. Large tomato, squash and zucchini plants act like umbrellas. The soil around their base can stay dry after a light rain.
  • Under trees. Tree canopies catch light rain, and tree roots compete for whatever gets through.
  • Covered beds. Row cover lets some rain through; plastic and cloches don't let any through.

The finger test still wins

After rain, push a finger about two inches into the soil. If it's moist, skip watering. If it's dry, the rain didn't go deep enough, and your plants need a drink even though everything looks wet. Check again each morning until the top two inches start to dry out.

When to water even though it rained

  • In a heat wave. At 90°F and up, plants can use up a half inch of rain in a day or two.
  • Right after planting. New seedlings and fresh seeds have tiny roots near the surface. Keep the top layer moist until they're established.
  • On hard, dry clay. A sudden downpour on dry clay mostly runs off. Water slowly first to help it soak in, or add mulch to stop the runoff.

When rain is coming

If a good soaking is forecast for tomorrow and it isn't very hot, you can usually skip watering today and let the rain do it. Your plants will be fine for a day. If it's a hot day, or the forecast is only a light chance of showers, water anyway. A missed rain plus a skipped watering on a hot day is how plants get stressed.

Too much rain

A long wet spell brings the opposite problem. Waterlogged soil pushes air out from around the roots, and plants can wilt or yellow even though they're sitting in water. Raised beds and soil with plenty of compost drain best. If puddles sit in your bed for more than a day after rain, it's worth improving the drainage before next season.

The short version

A quarter inch of rain doesn't count, half an inch means skip watering for a couple of days, and an inch covers most of the week. Measure what actually fell, check pots and sheltered beds separately, and trust the two-inch finger test over the sky.
